AN EDITORIAL ANALYSIS BASED ON SOURCED REPORTING
THE BIG STORY

The maker of the machines.

The departure of Jensen Huang from Nvidia asks a question that reaches far beyond one company's boardroom: what happens when the infrastructure of an age has been built around one person?

For more than three decades, Jensen Huang and Nvidia were nearly impossible to separate. The company he helped found became a defining supplier of the artificial-intelligence boom. Its processors and systems power the technology that other businesses are betting their futures on.

On November 12, Nvidia announced that Huang had resigned as president, chief executive, and a director. Chief financial officer Colette Kress was appointed interim CEO, and Harvey C. Jones was named chair of the board. The company said it would consider internal and external candidates for the permanent role.

The facts of the transition are clear. What it means for the future of artificial intelligence is far less certain.

A company and a character.

In technology, founders can become something more than managers. They give a company its language, ambition, appetite for risk, and identity. Customers buy a product; investors buy a theory about its future. Often, the founder is the person telling that story.

The problem is that institutions must eventually become larger than their originators. Nvidia's products, research, partnerships, and finances belong to a vast organization. Yet its public story has long been inseparable from its charismatic leader. The company now has to prove that both can survive apart.

What comes next.

Colette Kress has been Nvidia's finance chief since 2013. That history makes her a familiar figure to investors and an experienced steward of the business. But the questions awaiting her are not only financial.

Can Nvidia continue to set the pace in accelerated computing? How will customers react? What will a new permanent CEO preserve, and what might they change? How does a company maintain its ambitions when the person who articulated them is no longer there?

Those are questions for the coming months, not answers that can be manufactured on an afternoon's deadline. And perhaps that is where good coverage begins: knowing which claims are certain, and which ones belong to tomorrow.
